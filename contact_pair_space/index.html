A search space of touching scenes: contact pairs of a mug and a spoon

Abstract. We describe a way to write down scenes of a mug and a spoon on a table in which the two bodies always touch and never overlap. A scene is given by a point on the surface of each body, one angle and the direction of gravity. The spoon's orientation is not chosen freely: it follows from turning the spoon until its surface normal at its point opposes the mug's surface normal at the mug's point. Every step from these numbers to a scene is an explicit computation on the two point clouds. We then say what it means to sample this space uniformly, so that every place of the mug is equally likely to be the place of contact, how the sampling can be restricted to a region such as the inside of the cup, or to scenes in which the mug rests stably on the table or stands upright, and show sampled scenes and their simulated release in two interactive figures.

1. The idea

Place a spoon somewhere against a mug. To describe where it is, one could give the spoon's position and orientation relative to the mug, six numbers in all. But not every choice of six numbers is a placement of interest: for most of them the spoon floats away from the mug or passes through it. The placements in which the two bodies just touch form a thinner set, with five degrees of freedom, because touching imposes one condition on the six numbers.

This suggests describing a touching placement by where the touch happens. Choose a point on the mug and a point on the spoon and bring the two together. A point on a surface takes two numbers, so the two points take four. Bringing the points together fixes the spoon's position, and requiring the two surfaces to be tangent there, which is what touching without overlapping means, fixes its orientation up to one remaining freedom: the spoon can still turn about the common normal, as a compass needle turns about its pin. That turn is the fifth number. Two points and one angle therefore describe a touching placement, and the spoon's rotation never appears as a coordinate of its own.

Two further things are needed to turn this into scenes on a table. The pair of bodies must be oriented with respect to gravity, which takes a direction, and set down on the table. And a chosen pair of points may be impossible: the spoon may be unable to touch the mug there without passing through it somewhere else. The next sections treat these in turn.

2. What is given

Each body is known only through a cloud of points sampled on its surface, each with the outward unit normal of the surface there. The mug's cloud has 131,072 samples and the spoon's 32,768, spread uniformly by area. On each sample sits a ball tangent to the surface from the inside, of radius at most 1.5 mm, and the union of these balls, together with further balls filling the interior, stands for the solid body. Whether two bodies overlap is decided on these unions of balls. Nothing else about the bodies is used, and the construction names no feature of a mug or a spoon.

We call the mug body \(A\) and the spoon body \(B\), and write points and vectors of a body in a frame fixed to it. A scene consists of a rotation \(R_b\) and a translation \(t_b\) for each body \(b\in\{A,B\}\): the material point \(x\) of body \(b\) lies at \(R_b x+t_b\) in the world. The table is the plane \(z=0\), and \(e_z=(0,0,1)\) points up.

3. The state

A state is the list

$$x=(y_A,\;y_B,\;\theta,\;u),$$

in which \(y_A\) and \(y_B\) are points of space, written in the frame of the mug and of the spoon respectively, that single out a place on each surface; \(\theta\) is the turn about the common normal; and \(u\) is a unit vector, the direction of gravity as seen from the mug. A point of space is used to single out a place on a surface, rather than two surface coordinates, because an arbitrary cloud has no global pair of surface coordinates, whereas every point of space has a nearest place on the surface. The state has nine numbers, of which seven are effective: two for each place, one angle, and two for the direction \(u\).

4. From a state to a scene

4.1 A contact point and a normal on each body

The place singled out by \(y_A\) is the sample \(p\) of the mug's cloud nearest to it. On a smooth part of the surface the normal there is simply the normal of that sample. At a sharp edge, such as the rim of the mug, the surface has no single normal: a body resting on the edge can be tilted anywhere between the directions of the two faces that meet there. To represent these contacts the normal is blended from the neighbouring samples. Let \(p_1,\dots,p_m\) be the \(m=48\) samples nearest to \(y_A\), at distances \(d_1\le\dots\le d_m\), with data normals \(n_1,\dots,n_m\). The contact normal is

$$\nu_A=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m} w_k\,n_k}{\bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{m} w_k\,n_k\bigr\|},\qquad w_k=\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{d_k-d_1}{\delta}\Bigr),\qquad \delta=0.5\ \text{mm}.$$

On a face all the nearby normals agree and \(\nu_A\) is the face's normal. Near an edge the samples of both faces enter, with weights that depend on which face \(y_A\) is closer to, so that \(\nu_A\) sweeps from one face's normal to the other's as \(y_A\) moves round the edge. The same rule applied to \(y_B\) gives a sample \(q\) of the spoon and a normal \(\nu_B\).

4.2 The rotation follows from the normals

We now place the spoon relative to the mug, writing its pose in the mug's frame as a rotation \(R\) and a translation \(t\), so that the spoon's point \(x\) lies at \(Rx+t\). Two surfaces that touch at a point without crossing have opposite outward normals there. We therefore ask that \(R\nu_B=-\nu_A\). The smallest rotation that takes one unit vector \(a\) onto another \(b\) is given by Rodrigues' formula: with \(v=a\times b\) and \(c=a\cdot b\),

$$R_0(a,b)=I_3+[v]_\times+\frac{[v]_\times^2}{1+c},$$

where \([v]_\times\) is the skew matrix with \([v]_\times w=v\times w\) (for \(c=-1\) a half turn about any axis orthogonal to \(a\) is used). Taking \(a=\nu_B\) and \(b=-\nu_A\) opposes the normals. Any further rotation about \(\nu_A\) leaves \(-\nu_A\) where it is, so the normals stay opposed, and this is the freedom that \(\theta\) parametrises. Writing \(Q(\nu,\theta)\) for the rotation by \(\theta\) about \(\nu\), the spoon's pose is

$$R=Q(\nu_A,\theta)\,R_0(\nu_B,-\nu_A),\qquad t=p-Rq.$$

By construction \(Rq+t=p\), so the two chosen points coincide, and \(R\nu_B=-\nu_A\), so the surfaces are tangent there. The count announced in Section 1 can now be checked: \(p\) and \(q\) contribute two numbers each and \(\theta\) one, five in all, the dimension of the set of touching placements.

4.3 Removing overlap

The placement just built makes the two bodies touch at the chosen points, but nothing prevents another part of the spoon from lying inside the mug. Consider a spoon laid tangent to the inside of the cup: its two ends pass through the wall. To obtain a scene in every case, the spoon is moved along the mug's normal \(\nu_A\), away from the mug's surface at \(p\), by the smallest distance at which nothing overlaps.

That distance can be computed exactly. Let the balls of the spoon have centres \(b_i\) (already placed by \(R\) and \(t\)) and radii \(\rho_i\), and those of the mug centres \(a_j\) and radii \(\rho_j\). We want every pair of balls to be at least a clearance \(c\ge 0\) apart; the role of \(c\) is explained in Section 6. After moving the spoon by \(s\) along \(\nu_A\), the pair \((i,j)\) is too close when \(\|b_i+s\,\nu_A-a_j\|\lt\rho_i+\rho_j+c\). Split \(a_j-b_i\) into its part along \(\nu_A\), of signed length \(\Delta_{ij}\), and the part orthogonal to it, of length \(d_{ij}\). The condition becomes \((s-\Delta_{ij})^2+d_{ij}^2\lt(\rho_i+\rho_j+c)^2\). So the pair forbids no value of \(s\) when \(d_{ij}\ge\rho_i+\rho_j+c\), and otherwise exactly the open interval

$$I_{ij}=\bigl(\Delta_{ij}-\chi_{ij},\ \Delta_{ij}+\chi_{ij}\bigr),\qquad \chi_{ij}=\sqrt{(\rho_i+\rho_j+c)^2-d_{ij}^2}.$$

The admissible distances are the half line \([0,\infty)\) with all these intervals removed, and the spoon is moved by the smallest of them,

$$s^\ast=\min\Bigl([0,\infty)\setminus\bigcup_{i,j}I_{ij}\Bigr).$$

Sorting the intervals and sweeping upward from zero finds \(s^\ast\) exactly; no iteration to convergence is involved. It is instructive to see what the designated pair alone contributes. Its two balls are tangent at \(p\), with centres \(a=p-\rho_j\nu_A\) and \(b=p+\rho_i\nu_A\), so \(d=0\), \(\Delta=-(\rho_i+\rho_j)\) and \(\chi=\rho_i+\rho_j+c\): the forbidden interval ends at \(s=c\). When no other pair forbids more, the spoon is therefore moved by exactly the clearance and the chosen points remain the place of contact. We say then that the designated contact is realised. When the designated contact is impossible, \(s^\ast\) is larger, and the spoon ends touching the mug wherever the last forbidden interval ended. In both cases the result is a placement in which the bodies are exactly \(c\) apart at their closest and nowhere overlap.

4.4 Gravity and the table

It remains to orient the pair with respect to gravity and set it on the table. Let \(S(u)=R_0(u,-e_z)\) be the smallest rotation that turns \(u\) to point straight down. The mug receives the rotation \(R_A=S(u)\) and the spoon follows rigidly, \(R_B=S(u)R\), displaced from the mug by \(S(u)t\). The lowest point of a union of balls with centres \(c_k\) and radii \(\rho_k\), turned by a rotation \(R_b\), lies at height \(\min_k\bigl(e_z\cdot R_bc_k-\rho_k\bigr)\) above the body's origin. Taking the lower of the two bodies' lowest points, with the spoon's displacement included, and raising the pair by that amount plus the clearance \(c\) puts the pair's lowest point at height \(c\) above the table. Finally the scene is shifted horizontally so that the mug's centre of mass lies over the origin. Shifting or turning a whole scene about the vertical changes nothing physical, and this choice removes that ambiguity.

The first figure shows scenes produced by this map. In each the dot marks the designated contact point and the two arrows the opposed normals. The slider turns the spoon about the common normal, which is the coordinate \(\theta\), and the strip beneath shows at which turns the designated contact is realised.

5. Sampling the space uniformly

To sample the space we must say what is uniform. The requirement adopted here is that the contact should happen equally at every place of the mug: each part of the mug's surface is to be the place of contact as often as its share of the surface, whether it is easy or hard for the spoon to reach.

The two bodies cannot both be treated this way. The floor of the cup is 7 % of the mug's surface, but only the two ends of the spoon, 0.6 % of its surface, can reach it: a spoon 191 mm long cannot lie flat in a cup 82 mm wide. If the floor is to receive its share of the contacts, the spoon's ends must be used far more often than their share of the spoon. One body has to lead, and we let the mug lead.

The sampling is then uniform in two stages. First the place on the mug is drawn uniformly: \(y_A\) is drawn uniformly by volume in the thin layer of space within 1.5 mm outside the mug's surface. On a face this is the same as choosing a surface point uniformly by area; at a sharp edge the layer bends round the edge, and the wedge of space it fills there gives edge contacts, with all their tilts, a share of their own. Second, given that place, the spoon's place and the turn are drawn uniformly among those that fit. Write \(F(p)\) for the set of pairs \((y_B,\theta)\) at which the designated contact with the mug's place \(p\) is realised. The law is

$$p\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\partial A),\qquad (y_B,\theta)\mid p\ \sim\ \mathrm{Uniform}\bigl(F(p)\bigr),$$

together with a direction of gravity \(u\) uniform on the sphere. It is a conditional uniform law: uniform on the mug, and uniform on what the spoon can do at each place of the mug. It is not uniform over all fitting combinations taken together, since a place with few options gives each of them a larger probability than a place with many, the density of a combination being proportional to \(1/|F(p)|\).

The second stage needs no description of \(F(p)\). Candidates \((y_B,\theta)\) are drawn uniformly, \(y_B\) in the layer outside the spoon and \(\theta\) on the circle, and each is tested by the exact computation of Section 4.3; the first that realises the contact is kept. A candidate drawn uniformly and kept only if it lies in \(F(p)\) is uniform on \(F(p)\), so this samples the conditional law exactly. The price is the number of candidates tried, which is large where the spoon has few options. A place of the mug for which no candidate fits in 4,096 tries is given up; this happened for 289 of 40,000 places drawn.

5.1 Restricting the region

The same law can be restricted to a part of either surface. A region is any subset of a cloud's samples, and the place on that body is then drawn only in the layer over those samples; nothing else changes. Regions need not be described by hand. A body's pockets are the connected parts of the space that lies inside its convex hull but outside the body, and the samples that face a given pocket form a region. For a mug the largest pocket is the inside of the cup, bounded by 40 % of its surface, and restricting the mug's place to it gives scenes whose contact is inside the cup: 97 % of them start with the spoon in the cup, against 38 % for the whole surface. The selector above the first figure switches between the two.

5.2 Keeping the mug stable on the table

In the scenes sampled so far the direction of gravity is free, so the mug is usually tilted and balanced on an edge, and it topples as soon as the scene is released. A second restriction removes this. The mug may be required to stand on the table in a way in which it would stay if it were alone; the spoon is then placed against a mug that rests. Only the gravity coordinate \(u\) is affected, and the restriction combines freely with a region.

We first need the orientations in which the mug rests. Put the mug alone on the table, turned so that a direction \(g\) of its own frame points straight down. It touches the table at the point of the mug that lies farthest in the direction \(g\), which is at distance \(\sigma(g)=\max_{x\in A}x\cdot g\) below the mug's origin; \(\sigma\) is called the support function of the mug. Its centre of mass \(c_A\) lies \(c_A\cdot g\) below the origin, and so stands at the height

$$h(g)=\sigma(g)-c_A\cdot g$$

above the table. Rolling the mug to a neighbouring direction \(g'\) raises or lowers its centre of mass by \(h(g')-h(g)\). The mug therefore stays where every small roll would raise its centre of mass, that is, where \(h\) has a strict local minimum. These minima are found once from the shape, without any simulation. This mug has 24 of them, but most are facets of its many-sided wall, separated from the next facet by a rise of less than 0.5 mm, so that the slightest push rolls the mug on. Grouping the minima by the rise of the centre of mass needed to roll from one into another leaves 4 groups separated by 9 to 16 mm: the mug upright, upside down, and on its side with the handle down to either side. The deepest minimum of each group is taken as a stable posture, with directions \(g_1,\dots,g_{4}\). Released alone in each of them, the mug moved 0.2 to 0.3 mm in ten simulated seconds, which is its fall through the clearance.

The restricted map differs from that of Section 4.4 in two places. First, the gravity coordinate is replaced by the stable posture nearest to it, and the mug is set on the table by itself:

$$m(u)=\arg\max_{m}\;u\cdot g_m,\qquad R_A=S\bigl(g_{m(u)}\bigr),\qquad \min_k\bigl(e_z\cdot(R_Ac_k+t_A)-\rho_k\bigr)=c .$$

The second equation says that the mug's own lowest point is at the clearance above the table, whatever the spoon does. The mug's pose thus takes one of 4 values and depends on nothing else. The spoon follows rigidly as before, \(R_B=R_AR\) and \(t_B=t_A+R_A(t+s^*\nu_A)\). Second, a new thing can now go wrong, because the pair is no longer raised together: the spoon may reach below the table. Its lowest point is at height

$$z_B=\min_k\bigl(e_z\cdot(R_Bc_k+t_B)-\rho_k\bigr),$$

with \(c_k,\rho_k\) the spoon's balls, and the placement is kept when \(z_B\ge c\). Otherwise the spoon is raised straight up by the smallest distance at which it is clear of both the table and the mug, by the interval sweep of Section 4.3 with the vertical as its direction. As at a blocked turn, the result is still a scene without overlap, with the mug resting, but the chosen points no longer touch. A state is called admissible when the designated contact is realised and no raise is needed.

The sampling law keeps its form. The posture joins what is drawn in the second stage,

$$p\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\partial A),\qquad (m,y_B,\theta)\mid p\;\sim\;\mathrm{Uniform}\bigl(F(p)\bigr),$$

where \(F(p)\) is now the set of postures, spoon places and turns that are admissible at \(p\). Candidates are drawn as before, the posture uniformly among the 4, until one is admissible. Every place of the mug that can be touched in some stable posture therefore remains equally likely to be the contact. The base, for example, lies on the table when the mug is upright, and is touched with the mug upside down or on its side. The postures themselves are then not equally likely: a posture in which a place can be touched in more ways is drawn more often there. Over 20,000 sampled states the mug was upright in 29 %, upside down in 18 %, and on its side in 27 % and 27 %. The contacts again follow the surface: the outer wall carries 43.7 % of the contacts for 42.3 % of the surface, the inner wall 31.5 % for 33.2 %, the cup floor 6.7 % for 7.4 %, the rim top 3.4 % for 3.1 %, the base 10.8 % for 10.6 % and the handle 3.9 % for 3.3 %. A kept state cost 80 candidates on average, and 389 of 40,000 places were given up.

Every sampled state is admissible, and in the cloud model the mug stands exactly in its posture on the table, the spoon touches it at the chosen points, and the spoon's lowest point is above the table (600 scenes checked by an independent computation over all pairs of balls). The simulator reported no initial overlap in any of 40,000 sampled scenes. Released, such a scene behaves differently from one with a free mug. The mug stays where it was set in 91 % of the scenes, meaning that at rest its centre of mass is within 1 mm and its orientation within one degree of the start; in the others the spoon has pushed or tipped it, which the postures of the mug alone do not account for. Of 10,000 scenes, 6,471 ended static with the two bodies in contact, against 5,031 with the mug free; sampled inside the cup only, 9,433 against 7,799. A second seed gives 6,514 and 9,450. Fewer kinds of scene are produced, however. Sorted by the coarse description used in Section 6, the static scenes of the stable mug fall into 16 groups against 21 for the free mug, because the mug takes only 4 postures: scenes with a tilted mug, and most scenes in which the mug has to move to come to rest, do not occur. The selector Mug above either figure switches to these scenes.

5.3 Keeping the mug upright

The set of allowed postures can be narrowed, down to a single one. The case of most interest is the upright mug. With the upright posture \(g_\uparrow\) as the only allowed one, the decoding \(m(u)\) returns it for every \(u\), so the gravity coordinate has no effect left and the mug has one and the same pose in every scene: standing on its base, opening up. Nothing else in the map changes, and the sampling law reads as before with \(F(p)\) reduced to the spoon places and turns that are admissible while the mug stands upright,

$$p\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\partial A),\qquad (y_B,\theta)\mid p\;\sim\;\mathrm{Uniform}\bigl(F_\uparrow(p)\bigr).$$

What changes is which places of the mug can be the contact at all. The base lies on the table, and nothing can touch it except along its edge (18 of 20,000 sampled contacts); it is 11 % of the surface. Places for which no admissible spoon is found within 4,096 candidates are given up as before, and these now include nearly the whole base: 4,782 of 40,000 drawn places were given up, against 389 of 40,000 when all 4 postures are allowed. The remaining places are equally likely. Of the surface without the base, the outer wall is 47.4 % and carries 48.3 % of the contacts, the inner wall 37.1 % against 35.7 %, the cup floor 8.2 % against 7.6 %, the rim top 3.5 % against 3.8 % and the handle 3.7 % against 4.4 %.

The same checks hold as in Section 5.2 (600 scenes checked by the independent computation; the simulator reported no initial overlap in any of 40,000 sampled scenes). Released, 6,133 of 10,000 scenes with the mug upright ended static with the two bodies in contact, and in 3,989 of these the spoon rests in the cup of the upright mug, against 1,300 when the mug may take any of its stable postures and 347 when its orientation is free. The mug is still where it was set in 99 % of the scenes. A second seed gives 6,109 static scenes with contact. Sampled inside the cup only, 9,365 ended static with contact (9,386 on the second seed). The static scenes fall into 6 groups, fewer again, since every one of them has the mug upright. The choice resting upright of the selector Mug shows these scenes.

6. Sampled scenes are touching scenes, not scenes at rest

A sampled scene has one contact between the bodies and, in general, one point of the pair on the table. That does not hold it up. The second figure releases sampled scenes in a physics simulator and shows what follows: the pair topples, the bodies roll and slide, and after some time they settle, often with the spoon lying apart from the mug. With the mug kept stable (Section 5.2) the mug rests from the start, and it is the spoon, held by one contact, that falls.

Of 10,000 scenes sampled over the whole surface and released until they came to rest, 5,031 ended static with the two bodies in contact, where static means that no point of either body moved more than 1 mm during a further ten simulated seconds. Sorted by a coarse description of how the spoon ends relative to the mug, these fall into 21 groups, the largest holding 34 % of them. Sampled inside the cup only, 7,799 ended static with contact. For comparison, the same count for scenes built by an earlier construction, which lowers the spoon vertically onto whatever lies beneath it, is 3,024, the largest group holding 47 %; half of that construction's scenes start with the bodies apart, and 2 % start with the spoon in the cup. These are counts for one starting seed; a second seed gives 4,992, 7,803 and 3,094.

6.1 The clearance

The balls of a cloud are inscribed in the body, so their union lies slightly inside the true shape, and it does so most at sharp edges. Take a right-angle edge and the sample nearest to it, at distance \(x\). Its inscribed ball has radius \(x\) and touches both faces, and it leaves the tip of the corner uncovered to a depth of \((\sqrt2-1)\,x\). A curved part of the other body can enter the true shape there without touching any ball. The clearance \(c\) of Section 4.3 covers this: it is set to \((\sqrt2-1)\) times the 99 % quantile of the distance between neighbouring samples, 0.37 mm for these clouds. With it, the simulator reported no initial overlap in any of 20,000 sampled scenes. Without a clearance the bodies touch exactly in the cloud model and overlap in the simulator by up to about a quarter of a millimetre.

Several limits should be kept in mind. The space contains only scenes in which the bodies touch; a spoon lying beside the mug without touching it is not in it. One contact is enforced exactly, and asking for two or three designated pairs at once does not work as a coordinate system: scenes with two contacts form a thinner set still, so two independently chosen pairs almost never fit together (0.4 % of draws for two pairs, none of 2,000 for three). The map is also sensitive. Because the spoon is thin, its normal turns quickly as the contact place moves across it, and where a designated contact stops being possible the spoon jumps to another one: moving the contact places by 0.05 mm changes the scene by a median of 0.37 mm and, in 0.5 % of cases, by more than 20 mm. Finally, everything here concerns two bodies.
